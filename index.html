How far the radiation reaches, and who it treats

Particle range and patient archetype in radioligand-therapy (RLT) portfolio decisions

Consilience · September 2026 · Pre-release · Not optimized for mobile viewing

www.consiliencestrategy.com

1. Introducing RLT

Antibody-drug conjugates reshaped targeted cancer therapy by attaching cytotoxic payloads to antibodies. The payload choice (DM1, MMAE, deruxtecan, others) became central to ADC strategy as different payloads work for different patient populations (Drago et al., Nat Rev Clin Oncol 2021). Radioligand therapies appear structurally similar to ADCs. The payload is a radionuclide and the choice between β, α, and Auger emitters influences clinical characteristics, and hence which patients may benefit most.

The analogy is limited as ADC payloads act through mitosis or DNA replication. Radiation does not, and high linear energy transfer (LET) α and Auger emissions least of all: They produce clustered double-strand breaks, which are poorly repairable in any phase of the cell cycle. Dormant and slowly cycling cells, which survive cycle-dependent therapy and may seed relapse, are within reach of RLTs.

Until recently, the choice of radionuclide was largely settled by supply (see table in following section for an overview). Most approved and late clinical RLTs use 177Lu, a β emitter, because reactor-produced 177Lu supply was the first to scale. Hence, RLT portfolio and clinical strategy decisions have historically been driven by:

  • Tracer fit to tumor cells (target expression, internalization)
  • Remaining unmet need and non-RLT competitors
  • Patient fit with adverse event profile (renal, salivary gland, marrow)
  • Patient population size and commercial and regulatory implications (rare disease designation, sequencing in care pathway)
  • Supply chain and (radio-)pharmacy infrastructure

What rarely entered explicitly is the match between particle range and lesion size. However, 3 developments render this topic relevant now:

  • 225Ac supply is scaling and diversifying. Multiple commercial 225Ac sources are becoming available (NorthStar, BWXT, ITM, PanTera, TerraPower, Niowave, Ionetix), some already producing at scale, and capacity is growing. E.g., TerraPower projects a 20-fold output increase from its Philadelphia plant, PanTera is adding a second Belgian line at IRE Fleurus, and Niowave is building a second Lansing plant to fill long-term agreements with AstraZeneca and Novartis. Most new capacity will arrive 2028 to 2029.
  • 161Tb appears promising in first-in-human studies. Early results from the VIOLET phase 1/2 trial indicate 161Tb-PSMA-I&T performs at least as well as 177Lu in mCRPC, with a manageable safety profile (Buteau et al., Lancet Oncol 2025). 161Tb features 177Lu-like β emission with short-range Auger electrons.
  • MRD as regulatory endpoint is maturing. NGS-MRD, ctDNA, and ultra-sensitive flow cytometry detect disease with lesions below today's imaging detection threshold of ~3 mm. In multiple myeloma, MRD already is an endpoint accepted by the FDA; we expect more indications to follow.

At ASCO 2026, Novartis reported first results from AcTION (Emmett et al.), the first prospective trial of the α-emitter 225Ac-PSMA-617 in mCRPC. At the 10 MBq go-forward dose, PSA50 response reached 100% in chemo- and ARPI-naive patients (n = 11) and 56% in patients who had progressed on 177Lu-PSMA (n = 16). Novartis now runs 2 Phase 3 trials of the asset (AcTFirst in frontline mCRPC and PSMAcTION after Pluvicto), with Convergent and Blue Earth close behind.

The question now is where (else) α emitters work best. A key aspect of the answer will be geometric, i.e. which isotope's reach best fits which lesion size.

2. Linking physics and patient archetypes

Radioligand therapy delivers radiation locally, with a predictable radial dose-deposition profile (O'Donoghue et al., J Nucl Med 1995).

We will use R10–R90 to express particle range: the radial distance from the decaying isotope, over which 10% to 90% of emitted energy is deposited in soft tissue, so 80% of the total dose. For our purposes it is more informative than maximum penetration or mean path length. R10–R90 ranges of the 4 relevant isotope classes are given below.

ClassIsotopesR10–R90Notes
β (high-energy)90Y0.3–2.5 mmMacroscopic crossfire; dose dilution over larger volumes. Used in HCC SIRT (TheraSphere, SIR-Spheres) and historically Zevalin (CD20).
β (standard)177Lu, 131I, 67Cu0.05–0.7 mmLower linear energy transfer (LET); crossfire reaching across tens of cell diameters. The workhorse of the current RLT field — Pluvicto, Lutathera, others.
β + Auger161Tb0.04–0.67 mm (β) + nm-μm (Auger)177Lu-compatible chemistry. Adds low-energy electrons from internal conversion in ~10% of decays — additional damage in cells where the radionuclide is internalized. The retention that raises tumor dose also slows off-target clearance, so internalized activity in kidney and salivary tissue can become dose-limiting. VIOLET first-in-human signal in mCRPC.
α223Ra, 225Ac, 212Pb, 211At, 227Th40–80 μm (0.04–0.08 mm)High LET; dose confined to under 10 cell diameters. Approved (223Ra in Xofigo) and multiple in development across PSMA, SSTR2, FAP, GRPR, MC1R targets.

A patient archetype is a recognizable disease state, an indication and setting, with a characteristic tumor-burden profile (TBP): the patient-level distribution of lesion sizes and how it evolves through treatment. 2 stage IV mCRPC patients can fall in different archetypes by their TBP, one with bulky nodal disease, one with diffuse bone micrometastases. The appropriate isotope may differ accordingly.

Where isotope range and patient archetype match...

α and Auger emitter ranges are a match for micrometastatic and marrow disease. Their short range also becomes an advantage where the tumor sits within or adjacent to dose-limiting tissue, e.g., bone marrow. The mCRPC M1b bone-dominant, GEP-NETs MRD post-Lu, and DLBCL ctDNA+ MRD archetypes sit in this zone. If VIOLET data hold in larger trials, 161Tb may become a natural successor to 177Lu for PSMA-targeted RLT and other internalizing receptor programs, where the Auger component may provide additional benefit.

...and where they don't match

The main mismatch is standard β range for micro-lesions and disease located inside bone marrow, e.g., multiple myeloma, leukemias, and many lymphoma settings. The 177Lu-scale range (~0.7 mm) would deposit a substantial dose into hematopoietic stem cells in marrow, i.e., introduce toxicity. Why RLT has stayed thin in heme oncology is discussed further in section 3.

α emitters and bulky lesions may still be a match, if tumor penetration is sufficient, i.e. if a ligand can put an emitter within range of most tumor cells. Binding only needs to be even enough, and emitter range defines how even. A β-standard emitter reaching 0.7 mm covers gaps in binding up to that distance by crossfire; an α emitter reaches 0.08 mm (~8 cell diameters). These numbers are R90 ranges; clinically relevant range depends on tissue, dose, and other factors.
Alternatively, over cycles, radiation may strip the outer layer of a tumor and with each pass expose the next ("onion-peeling"). The effect appears plausible but has not been directly observed in the clinic.

Small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) is an emerging test for our framework. DLL3, expressed across SCLC and related neuroendocrine tumors, is a target currently tested in 3 radioligand programs: 225Ac-ABD147 (Abdera), 225Ac-ETN029 (Novartis), 212Pb-MP0712 (Molecular Partners). Patient archetypes in SCLC may fit α emitters well as the disease tends to be disseminated and small-volume. In SCLC, burden is quantified via total volume, not as a size distribution; hence it is not shown in the figure.

A note on fibroblast activation protein (FAP). FAP sits on cancer-associated fibroblasts, not tumor cells. α range covers up to 10 cell diameters, so it only reaches tumor cells adjacent to a FAP-positive fibroblast. β crossfire covers stroma-to-tumor-cell distance well. In any case, how stroma is arranged within the lesion becomes relevant, which is to our knowledge not characterized well. The pipeline includes 2 β programs (177Lu-FAP-2286, Novartis; LY4337713, Lilly) and 2 α (212Pb-PSV359, Perspective; 225Ac-RTX-2358, Ratio), so time may tell us more for FAP.

Recoil energy and chelator instability in α compounds. We quietly assumed the emitter and its daughters stay in the chelator and hence with the ligand indefinitely. For α emitters this is only the case up to a point, as α decay imparts >100 keV of recoil energy to the daughter nucleus (> 1000x binding energy of a chemical bond). So the daughter nucleus/ nuclei typically leave the chelator (Bidkar et al., Theranostics 2024). Consequences depend on daughter characteristics, as listed in the table below for the most common RLT radionuclides.

EmitterUnstable daughters (long-lived only)Comments
225Ac221Fr (4.8 min), 213Bi (45.6 min)both daughters redistribute
212Pb212Bi (60.6 min)chelator escape observed but not caused by recoil
227Th223Ra (11.4 d)Radon is bone-seeking
223Ra219Rn (3.9 s), 211Pb (36.1 min)typically stays in bone
211Atnone (211Po, 0.5 s)decays before it reaches other organs

Both 213Bi and 212Bi persist long enough to reach the kidneys and other tissue. Part of the 225Ac or 212Pb dose is therefore deposited there. In this respect, 211At is an exception as its 211Po daughter decays in under a second (sub-optimal 7.2-hour half-life and limited cyclotron production capacity have kept programs limited though).

Clinical relevance of the effect is still unclear. Free 213Bi concentrates in the kidney in preclinical models and case reports describe nephrotoxicity after 225Ac-PSMA-617, but toxicity has yet to be attributed to the effect described above (Hooijman et al., EJNMMI Radiopharm Chem 2024). For now, salivary gland rather than renal dose appears to remain the limiting factor in α programs dose escalation.

3. What is needed beyond geometric fit

Section 2 speaks to one question: does particle range fit patient archetype. Many archetypes pass, and a fit is where a program may start to make sense. What also decides whether a program will be viable varies by context, but 3 questions recur: how crowded an RLT area already is, whether a systemic target exists, and whether the delivery model works.

Crowding — the fit is obvious and everybody knows

In the figure above, the mCRPC and GEP-NET rows sit inside the α and β bands. Geometry was never an issue here, the target is validated, and the market is maturing. The only variable left is how many programs and where they stand.

A star marks a geometric fit, not a de-risked opportunity. Each carries a non-geometric gating risk. In DLBCL ctDNA+ MRD, antigen escape after CAR-T or bispecific therapy (CD19/CD20 loss) can leave the target absent. In HCC post-curative MRD, no high-uptake HCC-specific target is yet validated, and the cirrhotic liver narrows the therapeutic window. Geometry is necessary, not sufficient.

As this whitepaper is written, crowding of the proven fits is accelerating. In September 2026 the FDA approved Bexlutry by Curium, a 177Lu-dotatate therapy cleared through the 505(b)(2) pathway with Lutathera as reference product. Only 8 days later, Lantheus (which Curium is looking to acquire) received approval for Bravnetsa, another 177Lu-dotatate therapy, however through the ANDA pathway typically used by generics and hence rated therapeutically equivalent. Both obtained full US approval without running any clinical trials.

mCRPC is the most contested setting in the field: Pluvicto, ~16 β me-toos, ~12 225Ac-PSMA programs, of which 2 are in Phase 3. However, an opening is down the burden axis, in post-Lu and MRD-positive disease, where a shorter range fits.

In GEP-NETs Lutathera proved the fit. 177Lu-refractory patients is where α emitters should excel and 225Ac RYZ101 (Phase 3) and 161Tb TENET (Phase 2) could give us an indication soon.

Target — the fit exists, the target is unproven

HCC: In the figure, 2 of 3 archetypes sit far to the right. Range is less of a binding constraint here but finding a viable target is.

The only approved radiopharmaceutical in HCC, 90Y radioembolization (SIRT) fits BCLC-B multinodular disease on geometry and has a role in BCLC-C with segmental or lobar portal vein thrombosis (NCCN HCC v1.2026). However, our framework shows where a short-range emitter may enable future HCC RLT.

Prostate and NET RLTs also work well because of high PSMA or SSTR2 prevalence as a surface target. In HCC, the lead candidate, glypican-3 (GPC3), is among the few liver-specific surface antigens, but expression is uneven across tumors and patients. Hence both RayzeBio's and Bayer's clinical trials select for GPC3-positive patients.

Clinical trials in unresectable active HCC. RayzeBio's GPC3-targeted pair RYZ811/RYZ801 (Phase 1/2, 225Ac, NCT06726161) is the first HCC-specific systemic RLT in the clinic, now joined by Bayer's 225Ac-BAY 3547926 against the same target. Active unresectable disease may be the more immediate population to target by RLT, however we believe there's more to be explored in HCC.

AFP-rising MRD as HCC white space. NCCN considered adjuvant atezo+bev (IMbrave050) but declined to include it after it failed to sustain an RFS benefit; no adjuvant systemic therapy is recommended, so rising AFP triggers imaging surveillance only. A short-range emitter targeted to an HCC-specific target would fit this setting on geometry. However, we could not find any clinical programs, which may be due to the high adverse-event bar in apparently disease-free patients.

Melanoma, similar to HCC, has no validated target. 212Pb-VMT01 (Phase 1/2, targets MC1R), would best fit the in-transit and visceral archetypes (fit α range). Whether MC1R and the overall approach hold up in the clinic remains to be seen.

Delivery — fit and target exist, the delivery model does not

In the figure, DLBCL's rows fit β and α cleanly, and targets (CD19, CD20, CD22, CD79b) are validated. What has kept the space white is mainly delivery. Unmet need in post-CAR-T, post-bispecific relapsed/refractory DLBCL is high (short median OS). Hence, the following 2 archetypes deserve consideration.

Legacy in relapsed/refractory bulky DLBCL (post-CAR-T or post-bispecific). Lesions span 10–80 mm, where β crossfire fits. Zevalin (90Y anti-CD20) and Bexxar (131I anti-CD20), approved in 2002–2003, showed efficacy but failed commercially. That history may be one reason why the heme RLT pipeline has stayed thin (more below).

White space in ctDNA+ MRD DLBCL after curative-intent therapy, single-cell to ~5 mm, largely below imaging detection. This is where the geometry argument is strongest: a short-range α or Auger emitter deposits its dose inside the cell it binds (assuming internalization, which depends on tracer and overall RLT compound), while β crossfire would scatter into marrow. The targets (CD19, CD20, CD22, and CD79b) are validated in non-RLT therapies, and the population well-defined: rising ctDNA after curative-intent treatment correlates with future relapse (Roschewski et al., Lancet Oncol 2015).

However, no discussion of RLT in heme oncology would be complete without giving the reasons Biotechs have largely avoided the space so far:

  • Marrow reserve as cumulative limit. Patients may arrive in RLT clinical trials with depleted marrow reserve from prior lines of therapy. Short-range emitters have the potential to mitigate the issue, though.
  • Heme is crowded already. Cellular therapies, multi-specifics, and some ADCs are highly efficacious, targeted therapies across most heme indications.
  • Operational mismatch. Hematology and nuclear medicine tend to be organizationally separate in hospitals. Complex RLT logistics add to this barrier.
  • Zevalin's failure as default narrative. Development leads may have internalized "heme RLT doesn't work", though Zevalin's failure was driven by delivery economics and timing.
  • RLT in combinations may prove challenging. Combinations have proven powerful in heme; RLT may not as easily stack on top of current standard of care.

Some reasons may be structural, others are operational, historical, competitive, and ultimately addressable given the right asset and the right patient population. DLBCL ctDNA+ MRD post-CAR-T is a setting where each barrier may be sidestepped: a defined population, single course of treatment, no active competitor, marrow reserve less acutely depleted than in continuous regimens.

4. How to work with this framework

The framework adds a dimension to how RLT portfolio decisions are made, spanned by the following 3 questions.

  • Archetype fit. Which isotope fits the patient archetype best, and does the actual treated population match (trial today, real-world tomorrow)? Line of therapy informs some of this: first-line patients and salvage patients arrive with different TBPs.
  • Competitive density. How many and which programs focus on this archetype-isotope combination? 12 225Ac PSMA programs in mCRPC (2 in Phase 3), is a thicket, requiring differentiation. No Auger program in DLBCL MRD is either a wedge (first-mover advantage) or a signal (the field tested the barriers and stepped back).
  • Label versus archetype. What is the path from broad-label approval to archetype-enriched indication? Most current RLT labels are broader than the archetype the isotope fits, which likely leads to an underestimation of the true potential of RLT.

One caveat applies underneath these questions: TBP characterization varies in maturity by indication. PSMA-PET and DOTATATE-PET give some information on TBP in mCRPC and GEP-NETs (though only above the lower detection limit of ~3 mm). CT-based Lugano criteria handle DLBCL TBP similarly. HCC volumetric measurement is standard. For MM, despite a mature MRI pattern literature, per-patient TBP is rarely operationalized in trials. For melanoma in-transit, no standard exists. Where TBP cannot be characterized well, the framework's predictions are harder to validate, and harder to use in trial design.

5. Limits and Validation

Direct evidence for an archetype fit–response correlation

Direct evidence for improved efficacy or lower/ fewer adverse events where range matches size is still sparse. E.g., in VISION, the predictor of response to 177Lu-PSMA-617 was target expression intensity (SUVmean), not lesion size or tumor burden (Herrmann et al., eClinicalMedicine 2024; Armstrong et al., ASCO 2022). In NETTER-1, liver tumor burden did not change 177Lu-DOTATATE benefit (absence of a large target lesion (>30 mm) correlated with longer PFS, as expected for almost any therapy) (Strosberg et al., EJNMMI 2020).

2 factors explain the relative lack of data. First, historically, pivotal trials enrolled patients based on target expression (not lesion size), so are blind to TBP-specific signals. Second, both VISION's bone-dominant micrometastatic population and NETTER-1's hepatic-mixed population exhibit TBPs, which match 177Lu's β range well. Or in brief, these trials tested 177Lu in a size-appropriate population, not across a broader size range.

A more direct test is under way with LUTACT (NCT07054346, UCSF), a head-to-head comparison of 177Lu-PSMA-617 and 225Ac-PSMA-617 before prostatectomy. It is small (target of n=45) and in primary rather than metastatic disease. However, it holds ligand and patient constant while varying the radionuclide, and measures heterogeneity of cell necrosis in the resected prostate. Primary completion is scheduled for April 2027.

There are other, less direct indications that our matching principle holds. Dosimetry (see Appendix A) favors short-range emitters by orders of magnitude in single cells. Clinically, Feuerecker et al. (Eur Urol 2021) showed that 225Ac-PSMA produces responses in 177Lu-PSMA-refractory patients, and the AcTION Phase 1 trial (ASCO 2026) confirmed PSA50 responses in its post-177Lu group prospectively.

As mentioned in section 4, matching range to lesion size is a necessary but not a sufficient criterion. Once it's fulfilled, target expression matters for primary efficacy. Where we see our framework's main value is in identifying archetype-isotope combinations that pivotal trials have not tested yet: particularly in MRD+, and post-Lu short-range emitters should outperform. Indirect (and direct) clinical comparisons will hopefully be available soon.

TBP and the imaging resolution limit

The framework needs a tumor-burden profile, and imaging currently cannot supply one at the scale where particle range discriminates most. PSMA-PET typically detects nodal metastases above ~3 mm and misses most smaller ones (Ingvar et al., EJNMMI Res 2022). Next-generation clinical scanners reach 2 mm in simulations, still well above the whole β-standard band (0.05–0.7 mm) and 1-2 orders of magnitude above α (0.04–0.08 mm).

Disease at that scale is nonetheless real and carries prognostic weight. Pathology has classified isolated tumor cells below 0.2 mm and micrometastases between 0.2 and 2 mm; both are associated with reduced 5-year disease-free survival in breast cancer (de Boer et al., N Engl J Med 2009).

This renders the framework less directly applicable (today), not less valid. Selecting patients prospectively for range-matched arms isn't possible by imaging, so our reasoning belongs into portfolio decisions rather than individual treatment selection. Trials enrolling on imaging-visible disease test RLT in a size range where we predict limited separation between different radionuclides. Using MRD+, PSA90 rather than PSA50 for patient selection, and testing for depth and duration of response may allow to show the true potential of short-range emitters.

Out of scope

This framework does not replace:

  • Tumor control probability (TCP) modeling. TCP integrates absorbed dose, dose heterogeneity, and radiobiology. Our framework is geometric, TCP is biological.
  • Supply and operational considerations. We deliberately excluded logistics, treatment-site readiness, administration and reimbursement from our discussion.
  • Assessment of Non-RLT alternatives. This whitepaper compares RLT vs RLT. Non-RLT alternatives and how they compare to RLT is a different topic.

6. Outlook

In addition to the developments discussed in the Introduction, we anticipate 3 developments to impact the RLT field and influence the use of short-range emitters.

  • MRD-directed, and earlier treatment. As ctDNA and NGS-MRD become standard across mCRPC, NETs, MM, and DLBCL, treatment decisions need to be taken before disease is visible by imaging. Regulatory has started to move on MRD (marrow MRD-negativity became a registrational endpoint in multiple myeloma in 2024), but not ctDNA, though its prognostic value is clear (ASCO 2026). How to act on "invisible" disease remains a fiercely discussed question, which several clinical trials are looking to resolve (not further discussed here).
  • Dosimetry personalization at clinical scale. Per-patient calculations of optimal activity per cycle (accounting for target expression, biodistribution, cumulative dose) exist in early clinical trials (e.g., CHU Quebec's dosimetry-personalized 177Lu-PSMA Phase 1/2, NCT05896371). Routine adoption is still limited by quantitative multi-timepoint SPECT/CT capacity at therapy-delivering sites. A company building this capability around an asset could truly differentiate. For α emitters though, quantitative imaging during therapy is harder than for 177Lu, so α-emitter dosimetry will likely lag the β pipeline.
  • Diagnostic pairs aid in tracking TBP. "Theranostic" pairs (e.g., 68Ga PSMA-11 and 177Lu PSMA-617) characterize TBP within the imaging-detectable range: lesion count and sizes, SUV distribution, change over cycles. However, they are unlikely to lower the imaging detection floor to below ~3 mm.

7. Conclusions

Particle range matched to TBP is a relevant filter for RLT portfolio decisions. It may help choose the isotope for new programs/ in-licensing in particular for microscopic and MRD-defined disease where the shorter range of α and Auger emitters is most beneficial.

We discussed 2 white spaces: DLBCL ctDNA+ MRD against validated targets, and AFP-rising MRD in HCC against GPC3. Each also has its barriers, but also a true geometric fit.

As the supply, diagnosis, and endpoint constraints that kept short-range emitters niche are lifting, mapping patient archetypes, and matching isotope range as a first filter may prove powerful in surfacing more opportunities, leading to broader RLT application.

Appendix A — Technical basis

The figure rests on a few physical choices, set out here so the argument can be read against them.

Dosimetric grounding. The matching principle has support beyond geometric intuition. Lee et al. (Mol Imaging Radionucl Ther 2022) showed by subcellular dosimetry that 225Ac's absorbed dose plateaus near the maximum α range (~85 μm) while 177Lu's keeps rising with tumor size; the dose-per-decay advantage for the α emitter is largest in single cells and microscopic deposits and narrows as tumors grow. The modeled equivalent dose per decay favors 225Ac by 2,320× in single cells and 2,900× in 100-μm microspheres. Short-range emitters deposit disproportionately more energy in small targets; β emitters do better as targets grow.

The figure holds 3 physical factors constant. A full assessment cannot:

  • Vector PK. The radioligand's pharmacokinetics (tumor uptake, blood clearance, renal retention, target homogeneity) modulate everything else. Range matching is a first filter, not the final assessment.
  • Activity distribution within tumors. Örbom et al. (2025), in a preclinical autoradiography study, showed 177Lu-PSMA-617 distributes unevenly within tumors, tracking PSMA expression and proliferating regions rather than filling the tumor uniformly, with uptake in necrotic areas appearing only weeks after dosing. R values from point-source dosimetry idealize what real tumors don't deliver. Tumor penetration through poorly vascularized regions is a real constraint on dose delivery, particularly for larger lesions where central necrosis is common.
  • Tissue density. Bone shortens R values ~40–50%; lung extends them 2–3×. Soft-tissue reference values are used throughout; bone-dominant disease shifts effective range left.

Appendix C — Note on multiple myeloma

MM is the indication where the matching logic is most exact: both archetypes (diffuse infiltration and focal lesions) sit at cellular to short-range scales. 3 antigens are validated through other modalities (BCMA, CD38, GPRC5D), and no Phase 2+ industry RLT program exists against any of them; 1 academic Phase 1 is recruiting (225Ac-daratumumab against CD38, City of Hope, NCT05363111).

Acute unmet need is lower than in r/r DLBCL post-CAR-T. NCCN MM v5.2026 positions Carvykti at 1L+, Abecma at 2L+, bispecifics at 3L+, and linvoseltamab newly preferred at 4L+. The relapsed/refractory landscape is covered by these therapies; RLT would slot in for triple-class-refractory patients in 4L+/5L+, a smaller but real population. MM is the indication where the geometric prediction is cleanest, not the one where unmet need is highest. The 5 heme barriers apply.
